# Volume 7 — Texture and Materiality

**Status:** normative computational design theory  
**Olfactory texture and tactile correspondence:** `NOT TESTED`

## Construct

Olfactory texture is the perceived or imagined material quality evoked by an
odor: satin, wax, velvet, powder, cream, grain, suede, lacquer, fibre, mist,
dust, pulp, oil, smoke, salt, metal, wet stone, warm skin. It is crossmodal and
semantic. The nose does not mechanically touch the object, yet assessors make
repeatable correspondences between odors and shape, softness, sharpness,
weight, depth, temperature, and other sensory concepts. These correspondences
are meaningful design variables when defined and measured; they are not
literal physical properties of the liquid.

Texture must be separated from note identity. “Sandalwood,” “rose,” “vanilla,”
and “leather” are object or family terms that may evoke several textures.
“Creamy” can arise in floral flesh, wood, gourmand body, musk, or cosmetic
powder. The same material may feel waxy at one ratio, dusty at another, and
merely masking in a third. Texture must also be separated from intensity.
Stronger is not necessarily denser, rougher, or heavier.

The doctrine uses **texture axes**, not isolated adjectives. Useful axes
include smooth/rough, dry/wet, crisp/yielding, powdery/oily, waxy/aqueous,
fibrous/creamy, polished/raw, cool/warm, translucent/opaque, elastic/brittle,
light/heavy, close/diffusive, and clean/animalic. A target selects the axes that
matter. Opposite poles can coexist in different regions or phases.

**Materiality** is the coherence of texture with object anatomy. Dry grain
belongs to wood when connected to fibre, body, root, or polish. Waxy petal
belongs to a flower when connected to living flesh and recognizers. Powder can
signal pollen, cosmetic makeup, dust, suede, or dry resin depending on context.
The texture gains depth from specific placement and relation, not from the
adjective alone.

## Mechanisms

Texture can arise from a dominant material character, a ratio between
materials, a contrast between regions, a configural blend, or a temporal
transition. **Surface texture** defines the first apparent boundary: crisp
peel, satin petal, polished leather, lacquered resin. **Body texture** defines
the interior: juicy pulp, fleshy flower, creamy wood, fatty skin. **Particulate
texture** evokes powder, dust, pollen, ash, salt, or grain. **Film texture**
evokes oil, wax, humidity, musk, or lacquer. **Void texture** evokes air, mist,
porosity, translucency, or spatial gaps.

Texture may be **reinforced** when several materials point to the same tactile
axis, or **articulated** when contrasting textures produce surface and depth.
Reinforcement risks redundancy; articulation risks split objects. A bridge or
shared recognizer keeps the textures inside one target.

Physical volatility can schedule texture. A crisp or sparkling surface may
recede to reveal wax, cream, powder, or grain. Adaptation can also change
apparent texture without major concentration change. Temporal texture is thus
different from merely adding a long-lasting soft material.

## Material translation

Floral texture can distinguish thin petal from fleshy petal, powdery pollen
from humid bloom, wax from water, green stem from cosmetic cream. White-floral
salicylate volume may be useful in one target and plastic-cosmetic in another.
The design must specify which anatomical surface it serves.

Wood texture needs more than a sandalwood label. Dry grain, splinter, cream,
oil, polish, root, mineral, resin, smoke, and fibre can occupy different roles.
A DHP-scale wood base may legitimately use many materials when these textures,
their time bands, and their flower or leather interfaces are independently
resolved. Multiple smooth woody materials that all enlarge one soft block do
not create the same depth.

Leather differentiates grain, suede nap, oil, tannin, smoke, powder, and warm
skin. Gourmand differentiates crust, crumb, syrup, fat, roast, steam, and dry
residue. Citrus differentiates oily peel, crisp zest, dry pith, wet pulp, and
waxy rind. Aquatic and mineral targets differentiate mist, water body, foam,
salt, wet surface, stone pore, metal edge, and dust.

Musks often alter tactile finish, but receptor variation and anosmia can make
their texture assessor-specific. Carriers and solvents can alter evaporation
or film; such physical effects are hypotheses until compared at matched odor
load and application.

## Interactions

Texture interacts strongly with identity and hedonics. A waxy flower can feel
more realistic or more cosmetic. Creamy wood can feel luxurious or flatten
grain. Suede softness can make leather intimate or turn it into powdery musk.
Wetness can create botanical life or shampoo drift. Therefore, every texture
endpoint must be interpreted alongside target fidelity and failure modes.

Texture also interacts with spatial perception. Powder and mist can create a
field; grain and metal can create edges; cream and skin can pull the odor
closer. These are sensory correspondences, not guaranteed delivery properties.
Measure them separately from projection distance.

Crossmodal studies support consistent odor-shape associations and semantic
factors including shape and depth, but they do not establish a universal
chemical-to-texture dictionary. Culture, language, familiarity, and context
can change labels. A project lexicon should therefore be anchored with
references and training examples while allowing assessor-specific terms.

## Failure modes

**Adjective stacking** assigns many texture words to one unchanged block.
**Material-name substitution** treats “sandalwood” as proof of cream or “orris”
as proof of powder. **Intensity confusion** equates louder with denser.
**Persistence confusion** equates long-lasting softness with textural depth.
**Cosmetic drift** occurs when wax, salicylate, powder, and musk erase living
anatomy.

**Texture homogenization** applies the same creamy-musky finish to every
family. **Roughness theater** adds harsh, smoky, woody, or leathery materials
and calls irritation texture. **Unanchored metaphor** uses terms that assessors
cannot apply repeatably. **Endpoint capture** retains a texture change despite
loss of the named object.

## Observables

Select a limited target-specific lexicon before testing. Record applicability,
intensity, location, time, and confidence for each texture axis. Ask whether a
texture belongs to surface, body, particle, film, or void. Record integration,
object identity, adverse drift, and liking separately.

Use reference materials or familiar odor objects to calibrate terms where
possible, but do not force consensus that hides genuine differences. Report
assessor distributions and repeatability. For musks and ionones, record
possible specific anosmia or unusual sensitivity.

## Controlled tests

Use a **texture ablation** with constant total volume and fixed object core.
Use **low/target/high perturbation** to locate the transition from absent to
supportive to dominant. Use **paired texture alternatives** when two materials
could produce different kinds of softness, grain, wetness, or powder while
serving the same broad function.

For multi-material texture, use elemental and mixture arms: neither, each
participant alone, and the combination. Hold flower, fruit, leather, or wood
identity modules fixed. Evaluate the targeted texture, identity retention,
spatial impression, temporal behavior, and liking. A texture improvement that
destroys identity is not a successful target-linked depth change.

Temporal texture can be measured with TCATA-style selection of multiple
attributes through time or with fixed-window ratings. TDS records the dominant
attribute and can miss concurrent texture, so method choice must match the
question. Repeat coded controls and balance presentation order.

## Claim boundary

Texture vocabulary and crossmodal literature establish that texture can be
measured; they do not predict the texture of a formula from its ingredients.
Modeled viscosity, vapor pressure, OAV, or material descriptors do not prove
olfactory cream, grain, wax, or luxury. Every finished-perfume texture remains
`NOT TESTED` until observed under a declared protocol.
