# Volume 11 — Nonlinear Mixture Behavior

**Status:** normative computational design theory  
**Formula-specific mixture interaction:** `NOT TESTED`

## Construct

An odor mixture is not the arithmetic sum of isolated smells. Nonlinear
mixture behavior includes receptor competition, antagonism, partial agonism,
inverse agonism, peripheral suppression, central mixture suppression,
overshadowing, configural blending, intensity compression, adaptation,
synergy-like enhancement, subthreshold modulation, and convergence toward a
generic object. These mechanisms explain why adding ingredients can increase,
decrease, redirect, or leave unchanged the information available to the
wearer.

The term **synergy** is reserved for a demonstrated interaction in which the
complete combination produces an effect not accounted for by the selected
component or lower-order arms under the same protocol. “Smells good together”
is compatibility, not necessarily synergy. **Masking** is a reduction in the
perception of one target caused by another. **Antagonism** can refer to a
receptor-level mechanism when supported by receptor evidence; in formula work
without such evidence, use the phenomenological term suppression or masking.

**Elemental perception** preserves recognizable component qualities.
**Configural perception** produces a distinct whole. Most mixtures are not
purely elemental or configural at every ratio. Changing concentration can move
the same materials between regimes. Expertise and instruction can also change
processing strategy.

**Overshadowing** occurs when a salient component captures identity or
attention. **Intensity compression** occurs when mixture intensity grows less
than simple summation predicts. **Genericization** occurs when added coverage
reduces distinctiveness. Weiss and colleagues showed that equal-intensity,
broadly distributed mixtures became increasingly similar as component number
rose, approaching an “olfactory white” under their experimental conditions.
This is an important anti-padding model, not a universal row limit. Fine
fragrance formulas are not equal-intensity random samples of odor space.

## Mechanisms

At the receptor level, multiple odorants may bind the same receptor with
different efficacy. One can reduce the response to another. Oka and colleagues
demonstrated antagonism in heterologous receptor systems, neurons, and
epithelium. Subsequent studies found widespread antagonistic interactions and
modeled their normalizing effect on population response. Human work with
beta-ionone and methanethiol showed reduced malodor intensity and
unpleasantness when mixed in one nostril, with weaker suppression when the
odorants were delivered separately to different nostrils, supporting a
peripheral contribution in that case.

At central and perceptual levels, mixture components compete for limited
representation and identification. Strong cues can suppress weak ones, while
adaptation to strong cues can reveal hidden components. Processing may preserve
features without preserving component identity. These findings make “I cannot
smell the material separately” an insufficient argument for either presence or
absence of effect.

**Configural emergence** depends on material identity, ratio, concentration,
experience, and context. In a ternary pineapple-like blend, a component change
smaller than one isolated just-noticeable difference altered typicality. The
mixture object can therefore be sensitive to ratio changes that are not
experienced as a louder individual note.

**Subthreshold modulation** is possible: Hummel and colleagues found different
brain responses to mixtures with a subthreshold component even when subjects
could not consciously distinguish the mixtures. This establishes that
nonconscious processing differences can occur. It does not establish that
every trace improves depth, texture, or liking.

## Material translation

High-impact odorants can dominate at very low active dose and need trace
ladders. Broad diffusers can mask anatomy despite apparently supportive
descriptors. Materials sharing receptor or odor-family space may reinforce,
blend, or suppress; chemical similarity alone does not determine the result.

Naturals already contain internally interacting mixtures. Combining several
rich naturals can produce beautiful integrated complexity or rapid blur.
Composite-OAV decomposition identifies candidate high-impact constituents,
but cannot compute the full perceptual interaction. Known chemicals used to
“complete” a natural must be tested at the exact ratio.

Musks and ionones introduce assessor variability through receptor genetics and
specific anosmia. A material may dominate one assessor's mixture and disappear
for another. Averaging can conceal this topology. Woods, ambers, salicylates,
sweeteners, and citrus terpenes can form large common pools that define the
whole even when many smaller rows remain theoretically present.

Carriers affect liquid-phase concentration and headspace but do not eliminate
nonlinear receptor or perceptual behavior. Dilution can change quality as well
as intensity because relative thresholds and suppression change.

## Interactions

Nonlinearity couples every other depth dimension. Identity can emerge or
collapse. Anatomy can be masked. Texture can change when one participant is
suppressed. Temporal emergence can reflect adaptation rather than evaporation.
Spatial identity can change with dilution at distance. Hedonic valence can
shift when an antagonist reduces an unpleasant cue or when a familiar sweet
carrier dominates.

Pairwise interaction maps are useful but incomplete. In an n-ary formula,
material A may suppress B, C may suppress A, and the complete combination may
restore or transform B. The program therefore separates pairwise hypotheses
from formula-specific hyperedges and blocks automatic promotion.

Dose dependence prevents permanent pairing rules. A bridge at one ratio can
mask at another. A shadow can support realism at trace and become the subject
at a higher dose. A high-count formula needs ratio robustness tests, not only
one optimized point.

## Failure modes

**Linear addition error** predicts the whole by summing descriptors,
intensities, OAVs, or pleasantness. **Synergy inflation** labels every favorable
pair synergistic. **Subthreshold mysticism** protects traces from testing.
**Pairwise closure** treats a list of good pairs as proof of a coherent formula.
**Count monotonicity** assumes more components provide more perceived facets.

**Olfactory-white overgeneralization** imposes a thirty-row cap from a
condition-specific experiment. **Model antagonism overreach** applies receptor
evidence from one ligand system to unrelated perfume materials. **Average
suppression blindness** ignores assessor subgroups. **Concentration freeze**
assumes an interaction keeps the same sign across dose and time.

## Observables

Record participant identities, stock strength, active ratios, matrix,
temperature, time, formula hash, and relation hypothesis. Sensory endpoints
include component detectability, target identity, typicality, total intensity,
component intensity, masking, emergence, texture, genericization, and liking.
For receptor-backed claims record receptor, assay, species, concentration, and
transfer limitations.

Computational outputs may flag high OAV dominance, effective component count,
distribution evenness, temporal cliffs, and likely saturation. They are
screening observables, not proofs of mixture quality.

## Controlled tests

For two participants use four arms when feasible: neither, A, B, and A+B, all
at constant total volume. For three participants use the full eight-arm
factorial or a justified fractional design preserving the focal interaction.
For ratios use low/target/high or a denser response curve. Freeze all other
modules.

Use triangle or forced-discrimination tests for detectability, descriptive
ratings for quality, and preference with ties for hedonics. A discrimination
failure does not prove chemical or neural identity, and a discrimination
success does not prove target benefit. Record both target and adverse
endpoints.

For genericization, compare distinct formula variants and ask both similarity
and target identity under blinded conditions. For hidden-component emergence,
use controlled adaptation carefully; do not assume ordinary wear reproduces
the laboratory adaptation protocol.

## Claim boundary

The literature establishes that nonlinearity is common and addition can
subtract information. It does not predict the sign or size of an interaction
in an untested perfume. Receptor data, OAV, descriptor overlap, and pairing
rules generate hypotheses only. Every formula-specific synergy, masking,
emergence, or genericization claim remains `NOT TESTED` until controlled
physical evidence exists.
